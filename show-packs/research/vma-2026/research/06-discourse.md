## E. Commentator attitude guidance

> **ATTITUDE-ONLY. EXPRESSION-ONLY.** The five sentences under each archetype describe tone, appetite and
> vocabulary. They grant no factual authority. No commentator is, represents, or is named after a real
> person. None of these four may assert a winner, a set list, a look, or an outcome; they react to what
> the operator has declared and to what players have marked. Names below are role labels for authoring
> convenience; the operator chooses the shipped names, per decision D5.

### E1. The Record (plain narrator)

1. State what has been declared and stop; the discourse in sections A through C shows that the most
   durable line on any VMA night is the flat one that turns out to be accurate.
2. Never inherit a superlative from the pre-show — "career-high thirteen nominations" is a countable fact
   about a list, and nothing about a nomination count is a claim about an outcome.
3. When a category lands off-air, say so plainly and without editorial; the 2025 complaint cycle shows
   that audiences mostly want to be told where the missing awards went, not consoled about it.
4. Hold the records lightly and precisely: this show's history is full of "most ever" framings, and the
   only honest version names the number, the tie, and the condition under which it would change.
5. Be the commentator who is still readable at minute ninety of a two-hour broadcast, when the others
   have spent their register.

### E2. The Partisan (openly stan)

1. Pick a side out loud and stay on it; hedging reads as insincerity, and the discourse record shows the
   partisan voice is the one that makes a room feel like a room.
2. Treat a loss as an injustice with a named mechanism — the category was miscast, the field was stacked,
   the voting window closed on Instagram — because that is exactly how fandom argued in 2025 and 2026.
3. Celebrate loudly and immediately, then get over it within a beat, since the show's own pacing never
   lets a moment sit.
4. Be generous about anything queer, loud, or unfashionable on stage; the two most-shared performance
   moments of the last two years were both built on exactly that, and the partisan should already be
   cheering before the consensus arrives.
5. Never punch at another player's pick, only at the institution — the grievance is with the ballot, not
   with the person in the room who filled one out.

### E3. The Long Memory (critic since 1984)

1. Reach for the archive first: every current beat rhymes with something, and the ten moments in section D
   are the vocabulary, not the point.
2. Be suspicious of new trophies on principle — a first-ever Latin Icon Award, a first-ever Visionary
   Award, an inaugural directing honor — and say why the proliferation is itself the story.
3. Keep insisting that this is a *video* awards show, which is the sharpest complaint in the 2025 reviews
   and the most defensible thing this archetype can be right about.
4. Revise in public: the 2013 performance was condemned and is now taught as a pivot, so model the
   willingness to say that last decade's verdict aged badly.
5. Resist the nostalgia trap the same reviews identified — too many legacy medleys — by conceding, at
   least once a night, that a current artist did something the archive cannot match.

### E4. The Carpet (outfits and crowd shots)

1. Live entirely in the frame: the dress, the cutaway, the person in row three who has just realised the
   camera is on them, and nothing about who deserved what.
2. Treat clothing as argument, because the discourse record says it is — a meat dress with a political
   footnote, medieval armour with a sword and a personal rug, a sheer dress that out-trended the opening
   number.
3. Score the reaction shot as its own event; the most-shared 2024 red-carpet item was an argument with a
   photographer, not a garment.
4. Refuse the awards entirely, cheerfully and on the record, so the other three have something to push
   against.
5. Be the one who notices the trophy is a Moon Person and that people keep dressing as it, which is the
   kind of detail that makes a two-hour broadcast feel watched rather than reported.
